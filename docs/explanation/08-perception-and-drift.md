# Perception and Drift: Acclimation to Environmental Variance

For an organism navigating a complex system, sensory input alone carries no inherent meaning. When an organism observes a metric—such as a CPU load resting at 80%—it possesses no innate knowledge of whether this state represents a flourishing equilibrium or an impending catastrophe. To survive, it must diligently learn to construct a sense of what constitutes a normal background state. As the environment evolves, it may undergo abrupt regime shifts or suffer from slow, insidious creep. The organism faces the profound challenge of adapting to these structural changes without succumbing to the constant distraction of minor, transient noise.

To achieve this, the organism relies on a sophisticated mechanism of statistical acclimation. Rather than accumulating an exhaustive, memory-intensive history of every single observation, the `HostAcclimation` subsystem utilizes precise online statistics. By continuously updating a running count, mean, and variance, the organism maintains a constant, memory-efficient $O(1)$ footprint that elegantly summarizes its historical perception of normality.

The foundation of this stable baseline is Welford's Online Algorithm, mathematically chosen to prevent the numerical instability that plagues naive variance calculations over long lifespans:
$$ \mu_n = \mu_{n-1} + \frac{x_n - \mu_{n-1}}{n} $$
$$ M_{2, n} = M_{2, n-1} + (x_n - \mu_{n-1})(x_n - \mu_n) $$
$$ \sigma_n^2 = \frac{M_{2, n}}{n} \quad (n > 1) $$
This calculation is encapsulated within the `CapabilityBaseline` class, which strictly limits its exposed interface to count, mean, variance, and standard deviation. By deliberately withholding any subjective threat labels, the architecture ensures that the sensory perception layer merely observes, never usurping the critical interpretive judgment reserved for the organism's higher cognitive layers.

When the environment undergoes sudden, catastrophic changes, the `DriftAwareBaseline` module is activated to detect abrupt regime shifts. If a novel observation drastically deviates from the deeply established mean—specifically exceeding a threshold of $z_{\text{regime}} = 2.0$—the anomaly is not immediately accepted. Instead, it is isolated within a temporary spike buffer. The organism demands confirmation; only if this severe deviation persists unwaveringly in the same direction for $K_{\text{regime}} = 3$ consecutive ticks does the organism acknowledge a fundamental `REGIME_SHIFT`. Upon this confirmation, the statistical baseline is radically recalculated using exclusively the data from the buffer. Should the anomaly fade before confirmation, the buffer is quietly cleared, protecting the historical baseline from contamination by transient spikes.

However, environmental change is not always violent. A slow, gradual creep might perpetually evade the $z_{\text{regime}} = 2.0$ threshold. If an organism merely updated its rolling variance in the face of such change, it would gradually, fatally expand its definition of "normal," eventually rendering itself completely blind to the mounting drift. To combat this sensory blindness, the organism relies on the concept of a frozen noise floor. During its initial period of acclimation, the baseline standard deviation $\sigma_{\text{frozen}}$ is permanently locked. As the organism lives, a fast Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (EWMA, with $\lambda_{\text{fast}} = 0.30$) tracks current sensory trends against the historically committed mean. If this fast-moving average deviates beyond $z_{\text{creep}} = 1.0$—measured strictly against that ancient, frozen standard deviation—and sustains this drift for $K_{\text{creep}} = 8$ consecutive ticks, the organism finally recognizes the subtle deception and flags a `DriftKind.CREEP`.

Finally, physiological efficiency demands that the organism prune redundant sensory inputs. If two sensory organs provide the exact same information, processing both is a needless drain on metabolic energy. The `AdaptiveSenseModel` actively monitors for this by calculating the Pearson correlation $r_{xy}$ between disparate sensors using a bivariate Welford accumulator. If the absolute correlation reaches or exceeds $0.97$, the organism determines that the signals suffer from collinear redundancy. In a brutal act of metabolic conservation, it disables one of the redundant sensors, permanently shedding dead weight to optimize its survival.
