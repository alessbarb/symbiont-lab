# Predictive Shadows

For an organism navigating a chaotic universe, survival is synonymous with the ability to accurately anticipate the future. Yet, in the Symbiont architecture, blindly trusting an untested, newborn predictive model is a profound cognitive risk. Before a novel hypothesis is permitted to influence the organism's active decision-making pathways, it must be subjected to a rigorous evolutionary crucible—a safe, isolated simulation running quietly in the dark corners of the organism's mind.

When the system intuits a potential new rule about its environment, it does not immediately weave it into the active cognitive fabric. Instead, it instantiates the hypothesis in a quarantined state known as a `ShadowPrediction`. Within this twilight zone, the candidate model is fed a continuous stream of live sensory data, allowing it to generate ongoing forecasts. However, its outputs are structurally severed from the main `CognitiveGraph`. It can observe the world, but it cannot act upon it, nor can its internal failures trigger systemic collapse.

To escape this shadow state and prove its worth, the infant predictor must demonstrate strict mathematical superiority over the most fundamental of assumptions: the trivial baseline. The canonical standard is mere persistence—the naive expectation that the next moment will be exactly like the last ($y(t) \approx y(t-1)$). As the shadow model makes its predictions, the organism objectively measures its performance by calculating the gain using the Huber loss ($L$) across accumulated samples:

$$ \text{Gain} = \frac{L_{\text{persistence}} - L_{\text{model}}}{N} $$

If the intricate mechanics of the shadow predictor cannot produce a strictly positive gain—if it fails to yield a lower loss than the simple assumption that nothing will change—then its complexity is metabolic dead weight. It provides zero utility to the organism.

The lifespan and ultimate fate of every hypothesis are governed by unforgiving mathematical windows. As the shadow predictor quietly operates, it must accumulate enough statistical proof of its superiority. If it successfully gathers a threshold of $N \ge 8$ independent samples demonstrating strictly positive gain, it is promoted to a `supported` status. The organism tears down the quarantine, gracefully integrating the proven model into the active architecture of the graph. However, if the candidate model struggles, it is granted a maximum evaluation window of $N = 16$ samples. If, after this period, its predictive accuracy remains inferior to the simple persistence baseline, the organism ruthlessly abandons the hypothesis, permanently marking it as `retired`. Within this ecosystem, a predictor is never promoted automatically; it must mathematically dominate the null hypothesis to earn its existence.

Crucially, the organism refuses to forget its mistakes. When a hypothesis is retired, the system does not simply erase the memory of its failure. The negative evidence—the precise mathematical record of its inability to achieve gain—is meticulously archived in the `EvidenceRevisionLedger`. This ledger spans across permanent checkpoints, functioning as a structural memory of failure. By preserving the exact parameters of the dead hypothesis, the organism protects itself from wasting precious metabolic energy repeatedly testing the exact same failed ideas in the future, provided the surrounding environmental context has not shifted.
